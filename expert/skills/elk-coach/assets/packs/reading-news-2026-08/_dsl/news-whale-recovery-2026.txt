--- A
Few conservation stories are as closely watched, or as fragile, as that of the North Atlantic right whale. The latest abundance estimate puts the population at roughly 380 animals, about twenty more than in 2020 and the third consecutive assessment to suggest an increase. Set against that modest good news is a harder number: only around seventy of those animals are females capable of breeding. For a species that once numbered in the tens of thousands, recovery at this scale is measured in individual births rather than in percentages.
--- B
The 2025-26 calving season offered the clearest encouragement in nearly two decades. Researchers recorded 23 newborn calves, the highest seasonal total since 2009 and the first time in twenty years that a season has set a record of this kind. Survey teams working off the south-eastern coast of the United States, where the animals gather in winter, logged more than 500 sightings involving 129 distinct individuals. That single figure represents more than a quarter of the entire population, and sightings were up about 29 per cent on the previous season.
--- C
Biologists pay closer attention to the gap between calves than to the number of them. Of the twenty mothers seen with calves this season, thirteen had last given birth in 2021 or 2022, which implies an interval of roughly four years. In a healthy right whale population females calve every three to four years. Through much of the 2010s the interval stretched to seven or even ten years, a pattern researchers read as a sign that the animals were not finding enough food. The shortening of that gap therefore matters more than the headcount does.
--- D
The recent history of the population explains why caution prevails. Numbers peaked at about 484 animals in 2011 before sliding to roughly 359 by 2020, and the current estimate of around 380 still leaves the species far below its size before commercial whaling. Where recovery has happened elsewhere, it has taken generations rather than years.
--- E
Mortality remains the binding constraint. The unusual mortality event declared in 2017 is still active: by July 2026 it had involved 176 individual whales, comprising 43 known deaths, 41 serious injuries and 92 cases classified as sub-lethal. Entanglement in fishing gear is the single largest identified cause, implicated in 111 of the cases, or about 63 per cent, with vessel strikes accounting for most of the remainder. More than 85 per cent of all right whales carry scars from entanglement, and many carry them more than once. Regulators have responded with seasonal restrictions on fishing and on vessel speed in areas where the animals are known to congregate.
--- F
The arithmetic of recovery is unforgiving. Federal scientists calculate a potential biological removal level of 0.73 animals per year, which means that fewer than one whale annually may be killed or seriously injured by human activity if the population is to grow at all. Because the figure is so low, a single additional death can cancel out a season of births. The formal recovery goal requires average annual growth of about 2 per cent sustained for 35 consecutive years, a threshold the population has not yet reached.
--- G
Other species show that the trajectory can be reversed. Humpback whales, hunted intensively until a global moratorium on commercial whaling took effect in 1986, now number roughly 84,000 worldwide, and the population feeding off the north-eastern United States was removed from the endangered list in 2016. The eastern Pacific grey whale, protected by the International Whaling Commission from 1947, was taken off the United States endangered species list in 1994 and had recovered to about 27,000 animals by 2016. Both recoveries took decades of near-total protection.
--- H
The return of humpbacks to New York harbour is the most visible illustration. When Gotham Whale began its catalogue in 2012, researchers identified five individuals; the project has now recorded its five hundredth. Between eighty and a hundred distinct animals are seen in the harbour each year. An adult humpback, roughly the length of a bus, can consume about 1,360 kilograms of fish a day. Researchers attribute the return to cleaner water, the recovery of Atlantic menhaden stocks and shifts in the distribution of other forage fish linked to climate change. The sightings are genuine good news, but the unusual mortality event affecting humpbacks has never been closed. Recovery, the evidence suggests, is real, uneven and reversible.
