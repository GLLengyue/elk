--- A
Antibiotics have an unusual place in modern medicine. They are the drugs that most reliably turn a deadly infection into an inconvenience, yet their power is quietly eroding. According to the most widely cited analysis, drawn from the Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance project and published in the Lancet, drug-resistant bacteria were responsible for roughly 1.14 million deaths worldwide in 2021, and were associated with several times that number. The same projection foresees that figure rising to about 1.91 million a year by 2050, a total of around 39 million deaths across the quarter-century, if nothing changes.
--- B
Bacterial resistance is not a single phenomenon but a family of them, and its geography is uneven. A study in Nature Communications in 2026 confirmed that six species account for the dominant share of the deaths that can be directly attributed to resistance: Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Some of these organisms are everyday residents of the human body, while others are acquired in hospitals; the distinction matters because the threat each poses depends as much on where it thrives as on how it evolves.
--- C
The burden is heaviest in the regions least able to respond. South Asia is projected to record the highest number of directly attributable deaths of any region between 2025 and 2050, at around 11.8 million. Sub-Saharan Africa presents a different problem: its surveillance systems are so weak that the true toll is likely higher than published figures suggest, because the region scores barely a fifth of the target for laboratory capacity. Data from high-income countries, by contrast, are abundant but come from places where resistance is comparatively contained.
--- D
The World Health Organization has responded with a second ten-year plan. The Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance, adopted by the World Health Assembly in May 2026 and covering the period from 2026 to 2036, sets a headline target of cutting deaths from bacterial resistance by ten per cent by 2035. It retains the five pillars of the original 2015 framework, but adds explicit accountability milestones and a financing mechanism aimed squarely at the countries where laboratory capacity is weakest.
--- E
The epidemiological picture the plan addresses is stark. When the WHO's surveillance system published its 2025 report in June 2026, drawing on tens of millions of laboratory-confirmed infections across more than a hundred countries, it found that one in six bacterial infections no longer responds to first-line antibiotics. Resistance had increased in forty per cent of the pathogen-and-drug combinations tracked over the previous five years. In South-East Asia and the Eastern Mediterranean, the share rose to one in three. The economic cost is likewise steep: treating resistant infections is projected to reach 412 billion dollars a year by 2035, with a further 443 billion lost each year in productivity.
--- F
A large share of the problem sits outside hospitals. In some high-income countries agricultural use accounts for roughly seventy per cent of total antibiotic consumption, much of it for growth promotion rather than for treating sick animals. The new plan obliges member states to regulate veterinary sales, to ban growth-promoting uses, and to adopt farming practices that lower infection rates without drugs. It also continues to emphasise water, sanitation and hygiene within healthcare facilities, a measure that reduces the number of patients who need antibiotics in the first place.
--- G
The pipeline that should be supplying replacements is thin. The antibacterial drugs in clinical development are dominated by derivatives of existing beta-lactam compounds rather than by genuinely novel classes, which means new approvals are unlikely to outpace resistance for the most critical threats. The reason is commercial as much as scientific: because new antibiotics are deliberately used sparingly to preserve their effectiveness, they are unattractive to develop. A handful of agents, such as lariocidin, offer new mechanisms of action, but they are rare exceptions in an otherwise stagnant field.
--- H
Responding to the crisis requires acting on several fronts at once. Stewardship programmes that pair diagnostic testing with careful prescribing have repeatedly been shown to slow the emergence of resistance. Artificial intelligence platforms are beginning to screen vast chemical libraries for candidates that bacteria are less likely to evade. And in July 2026 the WHO published its first blueprint on fungal disease and antifungal resistance, a companion strategy that recognises the threat from fungi sits alongside the bacterial plan rather than inside it. None of these measures is a cure; each is best understood as part of a wider effort to keep a diminishing resource usable for as long as possible.
