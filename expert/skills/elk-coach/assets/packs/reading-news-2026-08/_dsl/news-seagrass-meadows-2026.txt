--- A
Seagrass does not photograph well. It is a flowering plant that grows in seawater, and to a diver it looks like the green blur one passes over on the way to a reef. That impression is badly misleading. Seagrass meadows cover an estimated 300,000 square kilometres of shallow coastal seabed, less than 0.2 per cent of the ocean's surface, yet they account for roughly a tenth of all the carbon the ocean buries. Per unit of area they sequester carbon up to 35 times faster than tropical rainforest, locking it into roots and sediments stable for thousands of years. Along with mangroves and saltmarshes, they form the group of habitats now labelled blue carbon.
--- B
Their value is not measured in carbon alone. Meadows act as nursery grounds, and in the Caribbean around 70 per cent of commercially important fish species spend part of their life cycle among the blades; the figure for the Indo-Pacific is similar. The fish that divers travel to see on reefs, snapper and grouper and parrotfish, very often grew up in seagrass. Some animals depend on it absolutely: dugongs and green turtles are obligate grazers, eating seagrass and virtually nothing else. The habitats are connected, and damage to a meadow tends to surface later on the reef.
--- C
Roughly half of the world's seagrass has disappeared since the 1930s. One analysis put the loss at 7 per cent of remaining coverage annually between the 1990s and the 2010s, and there is little sign the pace has slowed; the United Kingdom has lost about 90 per cent of its meadows in a century. The causes interact. Dredging and reclamation remove meadows outright, agricultural nutrients trigger algal blooms that cut off the light the plants need, propellers scar shallow beds, and sediment washed off cleared land smothers what remains. A degraded meadow does not simply stop absorbing carbon; it starts releasing what it had already stored.
--- D
Restoration is technically possible and logistically hard. Unlike coral, fragments of which can be grown in nurseries and replanted, seagrass needs shallow water with a narrow range of light, sediment and salinity, and the labour required is large in relation to the area that can be treated in a season. The method now standard in British waters is to gather seed from a healthy meadow, place it in biodegradable hessian bags and press the bags into the seabed by hand. Survival is modest, around 30 to 40 per cent, but it has improved with each attempt.
--- E
Much of the work depends on volunteers. At Porthdinllaen on the Llŷn Peninsula, snorkellers move through an established meadow collecting seeds by hand, which are later planted at sites chosen in advance. The effort, run by Swansea University and the charity Project Seagrass with community groups, forms part of Seagrass Ocean Rescue North Wales. In one season more than 500,000 seeds gathered there were pressed into the sand at Traeth Crugan, in Bae Tremadog. Richard Unsworth of Swansea University recalls that everything about the site looked promising.
--- F
It was not. When the team returned to begin monitoring, the plants had gone. Oliver Thomas, a senior scientist with Project Seagrass, describes planning an elaborate survey and then finding nothing: one shoot near a marker buoy and, beyond it, bare sand. It took months to establish why. A marine heatwave had raised the water temperature by about 3.5 degrees Celsius over five or six days, a rate he calls unprecedented in marine terms and one that a young shoot cannot withstand. Colleagues now worry that intensifying heatwaves will make restoration harder around the British coast. The failure has changed where the team works, not whether it continues: a new site near Holyhead was chosen for being more resilient.
--- G
Water quality, rather than technique, is often the binding constraint. In Florida's Indian River Lagoon, damaged by nutrient pollution and algal blooms, replanting has mattered less than what happens upstream, since shoots cannot establish in water that will kill them; nutrient loading from farms and towns has to be reduced first. Where communities are treated as partners rather than regulated as threats, results improve: in the Philippines and Indonesia, fishing communities have proved effective monitors of the beds they depend on. Off Cu Lao Cham in Viet Nam, where coverage fell from about 50 hectares in 2014 to 15 to 17 hectares, a pilot begun at Bai Bac in March 2026 had produced, four months later, stems spreading beyond the planting bags and the return of goatfish, rabbitfish and snails.
--- H
Survey work still turns up surprises. Mapping by Cornwall Council identified a bed in Mount's Bay the size of 290 rugby pitches, larger than all previously known seagrass in Cornwall combined and equal to 3.4 per cent of the United Kingdom's mapped resource. Within the Fal and Helford, where roughly 100 hectares of common eelgrass remain, a partnership intends to restore 10 hectares within three years, with voluntary no-anchor zones marked by buoys. The recoveries are real, but they are slower than the losses, and the margin for error is narrowing.
