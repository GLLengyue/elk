--- A
Europe industrialised first and, on most measures, has less wild land left than any other continent. Its habitats record that history: the European Environment Agency reported in 2025 that 81 per cent of protected habitats are in poor or bad condition. The European Union's answer is the Nature Restoration Regulation, the first continent-wide law of its kind, which came into force in August 2024. It sets binding targets: restoration measures should cover at least 20 per cent of the Union's land and sea by 2030, and eventually every ecosystem judged to need them by 2050. Among its specific provisions is a commitment to return at least 25,000 kilometres of rivers to a free-flowing state by removing the barriers that fragment them. Member states are due to submit national restoration plans by September 2026. The urgency is not abstract: canopy mortality in the continent's temperate forests has doubled since the 1980s.
--- B
The regulation has not settled the argument so much as relocated it. In May 2026 the European Commission opened a public consultation on the much older Birds and Habitats Directives, part of a simplification drive that observers widely read as a stress test of legislation drafted in the 1970s. Farming organisations argue that restoration duties fall on those least able to absorb them. Defenders of the law reply in economic terms, citing official estimates that every euro spent on restoration yields between four and thirty-eight euros of benefit, from flood protection to pollination, and pointing out that degraded landscapes already drain public budgets more than repair would cost.
--- C
Behind the dispute sits an awkward question: restoration to what? The directives were written when conservation meant defending what remained. Rewilding proceeds from a different premise, treating ecological processes rather than particular assemblages of species as the object of repair. The Dutch reserve at Oostvaardersplassen, laid out on reclaimed land in the 1980s, became the proving ground. Ecologists there argued that cattle and ponies could stand in for the aurochs and tarpan that had been hunted to extinction, reinstating the grazing that had shaped European landscapes. The experiment produced a crisis of legitimacy alongside the landscape. When large numbers of animals were left to starve, animal welfare groups and farmers objected, and critics attacked the reserve's authenticity on the grounds that its fences and water control made it an artefact rather than a wild place.
--- D
Some recoveries are unambiguous. The European bison has recovered from a handful of captive individuals in the early twentieth century to around 7,000 wild individuals today, spread across several countries. The wolf returned by a different mechanism: not reintroduction, but legal protection, since the Bern Convention and the Habitats Directive made killing it unlawful and allowed animals to recolonise under their own steam. Both cases are cited as evidence that species can return once the pressure on them is lifted.
--- E
Public opinion is more ambivalent than the population figures imply. A survey of 10,000 people across European Union states, reported by Chapron and colleagues, found that respondents broadly favour the continued recovery of wolves, bears and lynx while at the same time opposing continued growth in their populations, and they largely oppose control measures that involve hunting. That combination leaves managers with very few instruments. The difficulty extends to money as well: a study assembling the compensation values written into legal texts across Europe found widespread biases in valuation across countries and very little standardisation in how the amounts were justified.
--- F
Restoration also has to deal with the animals already present. A team led by Oeser, which assembled one of the largest multispecies tracking datasets for large herbivores, found that moose, European bison, roe deer and red deer all preferentially occupied disturbed areas. The timing differed: for bison and roe deer, habitat suitability peaked immediately after a disturbance, whereas red deer and moose responded over longer periods. The implication for forestry is awkward, because the animals that recreate open vegetation structure also browse the seedlings that would regenerate a disturbed stand.
--- G
Human presence reshapes these interactions in ways that are easy to miss. Research by Ranc and colleagues on the golden jackal, which has been spreading across Europe since the 1980s, attributes part of that expansion to a human shield: wolves normally restrict jackals through competition, but where wolves keep away from people, jackals are released from that pressure, and modelling suggests that up to 75 per cent of the continent could eventually become suitable for them. Longer records point in a less encouraging direction. Analysis by Neff and colleagues of ninety years of insect monitoring in Switzerland identifies the middle of the twentieth century, when farming mechanised, as the start of a steep decline.
