--- A
For most of the twentieth century, statements about the state of the whole ocean rested on a scatter of ship tracks taken months apart. That has changed. Satellites now scan the surface every few days, and a fleet of autonomous profiling floats reports continuously from depth. What these instruments describe is a system under load. The ocean has become the planet's largest heat reservoir, and in 2025 the energy it stored rose for the ninth year in succession. Researchers estimate that the seas take up roughly 90 per cent of the surplus heat that greenhouse gases prevent from escaping to space. That absorption has slowed the warming of the air, but the heat remains in the water, and it is now re-emerging.
--- B
Ocean heat content is favoured by climate scientists for a specific reason. Air temperature is noisy; it lurches with weather systems and with the seasons, so a single cool year proves very little. The heat held in several thousand metres of water changes far more slowly, which makes it a steadier gauge of the Earth's underlying energy imbalance. Long-running archives make the trend visible: one maintained by the United States weather agency begins in 1955, and a synthesis drawing on Chinese observational data, published in 2024, traces the same steepening curve in the upper ocean.
--- C
Surface records broke repeatedly during 2026. On 21 June the globally averaged daily sea surface temperature reached 20.86 degrees Celsius, edging past the 20.83 degrees Celsius recorded in both 2023 and 2024. July was warmer still, at 20.96 degrees Celsius, the highest value ever measured for that month. The heat was not evenly distributed: during the first half of the year marine heatwaves touched about 82 per cent of the world's ocean surface, and in the Mediterranean roughly 80 per cent of the basin was classed as experiencing strong or extreme conditions.
--- D
Much of the surplus is being funnelled into a strengthening El Niño. The United States weather agency declared an event in June 2026 and put the chance of the tropical Pacific anomaly exceeding two degrees above normal at its November-to-January peak at 63 per cent, which would place it among the largest since systematic records began in 1950. Measurements from a Columbia University research centre show the progression: the standard Niño 3.4 index averaged 1.51 degrees above normal from May to July, rose to 2.03 degrees in July, and reached 2.7 degrees above normal in the week centred on 12 August. Below the surface, water as deep as 150 metres was more than 8 degrees Celsius warmer than average in places, a reservoir of energy available for further surface warming.
--- E
What happens at depth matters for decades rather than seasons. A 2024 study in Nature Communications concluded that warming of the deep ocean alone could make extreme El Niño events between 40 and 80 per cent more probable, and warned that the effect might persist even after emissions begin to fall. A review in Nature Reviews Earth and Environment reaches a similar conclusion for the upper ocean, linking the additional stored energy to rising sea levels, to stronger stratification of the water column, and to more frequent marine heatwaves.
--- F
The biological consequences are already measurable. Coral reefs occupy under 1 per cent of the sea floor yet shelter something like a quarter of all marine species; when water stays abnormally warm, corals expel the algae that supply most of their energy and bleach. A study in Science Advances combined satellite observations, shipboard surveys and genetic analysis of marine microorganisms and found phytoplankton showing signs of nutrient stress, with less chlorophyll relative to carbon than healthy cells would contain. Warmer water also expands, adding to sea-level rise independently of any melting ice.
--- G
For all the agreement about the global picture, the regional detail remains thin. A single planetary average conceals enormous variation, and although semi-enclosed seas and western boundary currents have been identified as hotspots, no complete geographic breakdown of the most extreme anomalies has been published. Layer-by-layer anomaly tables for 2026 have not yet appeared in the main repositories. That gap matters most to the people who must act on the numbers: coastal planners, fisheries managers and emergency agencies need to know where the heat is, not merely how much of it there is. Until that resolution improves, record warmth will remain easier to measure than to manage.
