--- A
Mangroves do several jobs at once. Their roots bind sediment, which slows erosion and takes the force out of storm surges; the mud they sit in stores carbon more densely per hectare than most forests on land; and their submerged roots shelter the juvenile fish on which a large part of the tropical coastal catch depends. Roughly a fifth of the world's remaining mangroves grow in Indonesia, which makes the archipelago the single most important country for any global attempt to protect or restore them. It has also lost a great deal of what it once had, mostly to aquaculture ponds and land conversion, so the distance between what is there and what could be there is unusually wide.
--- B
The proposals now on the table are large. In August 2026 a carbon project developer and a sustainable seafood company signed an agreement to build combined mangrove restoration and coastal livelihood projects, pairing carbon finance with a method known as Integrated Mangrove Aquaculture, in which ponds are redesigned so that mangroves and farmed fish or shrimp share the same ground. Their estimate is that around a million hectares of former mangrove along the coast could be turned from unproductive ponds into what one executive described as blue food forests. A scheme already under development aims to restore 10,000 hectares, generate roughly 140,000 carbon credits a year and support more than 2,000 local people, while improving habitat for species such as the proboscis monkey.
--- C
Money is the hard part. A review published in 2026 by a forestry research organisation concluded that public finance remains the backbone of early mangrove investment in Indonesia, channelled through national budgets, subnational delivery mechanisms and international partnerships, while market mechanisms, particularly voluntary carbon trading, have attracted most of the attention. The case for pressing on rests on what its authors call a triple dividend: climate mitigation and adaptation, coastal resilience and biodiversity, and improved livelihoods and social equity, with the last of these most likely to materialise where local communities help to design and manage a project and share in what it earns.
--- D
Regulation is catching up with the ambition. A draft rule for the marine and fisheries sector, prepared under a 2025 presidential regulation on carbon economic value, would require every project to complete registration, validation, verification and recording in the national registry, referred to as SRUK, before any credit is issued. The obligations do not stop at paperwork. Developers must set out how benefits will be shared, and coastal communities are to receive at least 50 per cent of net financial benefits, along with training, technology transfer and alternative livelihoods. This is not confined to credits sold abroad: any project in the sector must comply. Consent has to be free, prior and informed, environmental and social assessments are compulsory, and boundaries must be checked by coordinate so that two projects cannot claim the same stretch of coast.
--- E
Selling to foreign buyers adds a further layer. Transfers under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement require government authorisation and a corresponding adjustment to the national inventory, and the government has said it will establish a scientific panel to write the methodologies on which those trades depend. Alongside it sits a nesting framework, targeted for completion within twelve to eighteen months, intended to let project-level credits sit inside the national accounts. Nesting exists to solve a specific problem: without it, the same reduction can be counted twice, once by the project and once by the country.
--- F
Whether any of this produces a market is another question. Activity on the country's carbon exchange rose for a second consecutive month in July 2026, but volumes remained well below the highs set earlier in the year, and domestic trading remains thin. Rule-making is therefore running well ahead of the demand that would justify it, and the buyers the new infrastructure is being built for are largely outside the country. Whether the scientific panel adopts methodologies already in international use or writes Indonesian ones from scratch will determine how much existing project design survives the change.
--- G
At project level the obstacles are unglamorous. Carbon rights and the authority to sell them are not clearly defined, which makes investors cautious. Measurement, reporting and verification is expensive and its credibility uneven, yet it is what decides whether a credit can be sold at all. Negotiating with communities calls for skills that neither developers nor officials necessarily have, and benefit-sharing arrangements that look fair on paper can fail in practice. Other ministries are moving at different speeds: forestry already has an operating regulation, while agriculture and energy are still drafting theirs.
