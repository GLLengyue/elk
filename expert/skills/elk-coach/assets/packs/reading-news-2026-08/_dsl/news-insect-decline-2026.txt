--- A
The evidence that first caught public attention came from a windscreen. Drivers had noticed for years that summer journeys no longer ended with the glass speckled with dead flies, and in Germany a team of researchers eventually put a number on the impression. Working in protected areas over 27 years, they recorded a seasonal decline of 76 per cent in the biomass of flying insects, rising to 82 per cent in midsummer. The result was reported around the world as a collapse in insect numbers. It was not that. The traps in that study were weighed, not counted, so what they measured was mass: how much insect there was, not how many individuals and not how many species.
--- B
That distinction matters, and specialists were quick to press it. An entomologist at a British research institute that has surveyed insects since 1964 observed that the loss could have been driven by one or two relatively large groups, citing work at a second site where a single large fly species fell away while other groups showed no significant change. A further critic pointed out that most of the German sites were sampled only once across the 27 years, and that although the plots carried protected status they were small and already degraded. She nonetheless accepted that the measured loss of biomass was extremely high, and drew a harder conclusion from it: land that cannot hold flying insects is not of high enough quality, and better habitat has to be brought back into farmland.
--- C
Longer records complicate the picture rather than settle it. An analysis of insect monitoring extending across roughly nine decades in Switzerland found that decline was not constant through time and differed between ecological groups. The sharpest changes clustered in the middle of the twentieth century; some beetle communities stabilised or partly recovered afterwards, while butterflies showed more persistent losses. A record of that length is valuable precisely because it shows the trend is not a straight line downwards, since conservation, land management, climate and the traits of the animals themselves can all turn a local curve.
--- D
Two further British series point in the same direction with less drama. Forty-two years of sampling in cereal fields in the south of England found that twelve insect species declined and two increased, with some groups recovering before falling again, producing an overall fall of 35 per cent in the total number of insects. At a second institution, three of four traps showed downward trends in biomass over thirty years to 2002, but the decline was statistically significant in only one of them. Both studies covered a narrow range of habitats, and neither could say how representative its results were of the country as a whole.
--- E
What was missing for a long time was evidence from outside Europe dense enough to stand on its own. A study published in Science in 2025 assembled more than twelve million butterfly observations from monitoring programmes across the contiguous United States and estimated that total abundance fell by about 22 per cent between 2000 and 2020. The median species declined by 2.6 per cent a year, which compounds into a far larger loss across two decades. Individual species varied enormously and a small number increased, but the national pattern showed that the decline was not an artefact of a few well-known European datasets.
--- F
Freshwater insects refuse to fit the story at all. Several broad analyses have found average increases in abundance over recent decades, although this reflects better water quality and restoration in some rivers rather than a general recovery, and other freshwater systems continue to deteriorate. The contrast is scientifically useful, because it demonstrates that these populations respond when conditions improve. A review in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences described the terrestrial pattern as death by a thousand cuts, on the grounds that no single stressor explains every case. The stakes are not abstract: roughly 90 per cent of wild flowering plants depend at least partly on animal pollination, and more than three-quarters of food crops benefit from or rely on it.
--- G
That combination of diffuse causes is what makes the policy problem hard. A white paper published in June 2026, written by 135 researchers and synthesising eight European research projects, argued that wild pollinators face interacting pressures from land-use change, pesticides, climate change, reactive nitrogen and artificial light, and that fragmented and sometimes contradictory rules are undermining restoration. It also challenged a popular remedy. Wildflower strips give adults somewhere to feed, but they do not supply the nesting sites and larval host plants, such as nettles and wild grasses, that young insects require, and where those are cleared away the strips risk becoming traps. The report set out fifteen recommendations, among them cutting pesticide use and building pollinator stewardship into agriculture, trade, planning and finance rather than leaving it to environmental policy alone.
