--- A
Hydrogen was supposed to clean up the parts of the economy that electricity cannot easily reach: steel furnaces, fertiliser plants, the engines of long-haul ships. In 2026 the industry is discovering how wide the gap can be between a climate ambition and a signed order book. By the middle of the year, developers had publicly abandoned around 50 clean-hydrogen projects, and analysts believe the true figure is higher. Executives keep returning to the same two explanations: the cost of making the fuel, and the near-total absence of customers willing to sign the long contracts that would justify building anything.
--- B
The output figures are sobering. Global production of green hydrogen is on course to reach roughly 1.8 million tonnes this year, which is less than 2 per cent of the hydrogen the world already consumes, almost all of it still made from fossil gas. Even the more optimistic forecasts for 2030 put output at about 5.5 million tonnes, well short of the 25 million tonnes that governments had collectively targeted. For an industry that was supposed to be expanding exponentially, the trajectory now looks closer to flat.
--- C
The technology itself is largely ready. Water electrolysis sits at the upper end of the technology readiness scale, but it produces hydrogen at between $2.50 and $7.00 per kilogram, two to five times the cost of hydrogen stripped from natural gas. Two factors drive that figure: the electrolysers themselves, which cost between $500 and $1,800 per kilowatt of electrical capacity, and the dedicated renewable electricity that accounts for 60 to 70 per cent of the final price. Each kilogram of hydrogen requires roughly 50-55 kilowatt-hours of electricity, so the price of power dominates everything else. Fewer than one in ten announced projects ever reach a final investment decision.
--- D
Public money is retreating as well. Governments earmarked about 222 billion dollars for blue and green hydrogen in 2025, roughly a fifth less than the year before, with much of the withdrawal traced to Washington, where support fell from around 90 billion dollars to 28 billion after legislation trimmed subsidies and pushed the deadline for starting construction to the end of 2027. Where funding survives it is changing colour: improved tax credits for capturing carbon dioxide have quietly made hydrogen from natural gas more attractive, and some analysts now expect more than nine in every ten tonnes of clean hydrogen produced by 2030 to be blue rather than green.
--- E
Corporate withdrawals have followed. Shell cancelled a large Norwegian project in July 2026, citing weak demand and high costs. Woodside Energy halted a one billion Australian dollar plant in Western Australia and a 1.7 gigawatt scheme in Tasmania, abandoned a further project in Oklahoma, and in June 2026 switched the Western Australian site to a gas-reforming route that produces hydrogen for between one and two dollars a kilogram. BP paused a blue hydrogen plant in Indiana, and authorities in Canada withdrew land reservations for three large schemes. European producers have reported costs of around 8 euros per kilogram, against roughly 2 euros for hydrogen made from natural gas.
--- F
The projects that survive tend to have one thing in common. The 8.4 billion dollar plant under construction at NEOM in Saudi Arabia was 80 per cent complete by February 2026, and it proceeded because a thirty-year exclusive offtake agreement with Air Products guarantees a buyer for everything it produces. The lesson bankers draw is that bankability in this industry comes less from the technology than from a contracted customer; a cheaper electrolyser cannot compensate for an empty order book.
--- G
China is the conspicuous exception, and it has folded hydrogen into its latest five-year plan. It is turning out electrolysers at roughly a quarter of Western prices, and by the end of 2025 its hydrogen production capacity exceeded 51 million tonnes a year, with more than half of the world's renewable-based hydrogen capacity. By June 2026 more than 1.4 million tonnes a year of renewable hydrogen capacity had been built or was under construction, of which just over 270,000 tonnes was operating. In the wind and solar belts of Inner Mongolia, Gansu and Xinjiang, power costs about 0.15 yuan per kilowatt-hour.
--- H
Other developers have simply moved on. Masdar, the Abu Dhabi renewables company, cancelled a 100 megawatt electrolyser intended to supply green steel maker Emsteel, and redirected capital and solar capacity towards data centres instead; a small pilot plant built with Emsteel continues to run. If the last decade of solar panels and batteries is any guide, the decisive question may not be whether green hydrogen eventually scales but whose factories are standing when it does.
