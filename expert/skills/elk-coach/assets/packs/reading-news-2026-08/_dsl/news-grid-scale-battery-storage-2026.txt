--- A
In the first quarter of 2026 the United States added about 8.4 gigawatt-hours of storage to its grid, some 54 per cent above the previous record for the period. Cumulative capacity crossed 100 gigawatt-hours in the second quarter, and total installed storage passed 52 gigawatts after 8.3 gigawatts arrived in a single half-year, with more than 54 gigawatts queued behind it. Storage is no longer an accessory bolted on to renewable projects. It has become the standard companion to new solar capacity, and increasingly the thing that decides whether a solar plant is built at all.
--- B
The reason is price. BloombergNEF has surveyed lithium-ion pack prices since 2010, when they stood at $1,191 per kilowatt-hour; by 2023 the figure was $139, in 2024 it was $115 and in 2025 it was $108, a fall of 93 per cent in fifteen years. The 2025 survey contained a less noticed result buried in its segment tables: packs destined for stationary storage fell to $70 per kilowatt-hour, 45 per cent below the previous year, making grid batteries the cheapest application for a lithium cell anywhere. At that level a four-hour battery can undercut a new gas peaking plant on levelised cost in most American markets.
--- C
Choosing what to build is now a question of chemistry. Developers procuring in 2026 are largely standardising on lithium iron phosphate, which offers two to four hours of discharge, six to ten thousand cycles and a system cost of $130-160 per kilowatt-hour, and which has become the default where four-hour duration is required. Nickel manganese cobalt, at $160-200 and three to five thousand cycles, is losing share. Vanadium flow batteries store for six to twelve hours and survive fifteen to twenty thousand cycles, but at $250-400 per kilowatt-hour they remain a niche. Sodium-ion, projected below $130, is still at an early commercial stage.
--- D
Where the capacity goes is determined less by policy than by queues and land. Three states account for about 80 per cent of planned American additions in 2026: Texas, with 12.9 gigawatts, California with 3.4 and Arizona with 3.2. Texas leads because its market is the one large system where an interconnection decision arrives on something close to a financing timetable, and because West Texas offers flat, cheap, permittable land beside existing transmission. California pays a higher price per megawatt-hour but requires four hours of duration, which makes each megawatt dearer, and its route to a signed agreement is considerably harder.
--- E
Revenue comes from several directions at once. Operators earn from capacity markets, from buying cheap power and selling it dear, and from ancillary services that keep the grid stable, and increasingly they co-locate batteries with solar plants so that the two share a connection. The fastest-growing customer, however, is the data centre. Hyperscale operators have shown themselves willing to sign fifteen-year contracts for firm power: the Bellefield project in Kern County, California, was built on a fifteen-year agreement with Amazon and will reach 2,000 megawatts when its second phase is finished.
--- F
Almost all of this capacity is short. Most grid batteries deliver between one and four hours, which is enough to move solar output into the evening peak but not enough to cover several consecutive days of low wind. Long-duration technologies aim at that gap. In March 2026 Form Energy and FuturEnergy Ireland announced plans for a 10 megawatt, 1,000 megawatt-hour iron-air battery in County Donegal, capable of discharging for up to 100 hours at roughly a tenth of the cost per kilowatt-hour of lithium-ion. Advocates argue that such systems are what a wind-heavy grid needs when the wind stops for days.
--- G
The binding constraint has moved. Chemistry and cost are no longer the obstacles they were; permitting, interconnection queues and trade policy are. Developers in the United States face tariffs on imported battery components that tighten margins for anyone still negotiating supply, and connection backlogs that can delay a finished project by years. In Europe the picture is similar: SolarPower Europe argued in June 2026 that the binding constraint is the grid-connection queue rather than project economics, and called for a dedicated action plan to clear it.
--- H
How much further costs fall is contested. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory's mid-case projection for a complete four-hour system is $308 per kilowatt-hour in 2026, falling to $243 by 2035 and $178 by 2050, a far gentler slope than the one the industry has just travelled. The residential segment, unusually, is expected to contract by about 5 per cent in 2026 even though it recorded 1.3 gigawatt-hours in the first quarter, largely because buyers pulled purchases forward before a tax credit expired. The technology has won its argument; the remaining question is how quickly paperwork and copper can keep up.
