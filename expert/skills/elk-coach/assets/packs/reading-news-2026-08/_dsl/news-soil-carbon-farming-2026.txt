TITLE: The Difficulty of Counting Carbon in Soil
URL: https://www.ccarbon.info/news/isometric-launched-a-new-certified-protocol-for-improved-soil-management/

--- A
Agriculture is usually discussed as a source of emissions, but the ground beneath it is the largest store of carbon on land. The world's soils hold close to three trillion tonnes of carbon, roughly three times the amount present in the atmosphere, and they accumulate it naturally as plants draw carbon dioxide from the air and pass it below ground through their roots. The question now occupying researchers and carbon markets alike is whether management can accelerate that process. Estimates suggest that better management of croplands and grasslands could remove up to 430 million tonnes of carbon dioxide from the air each year, which is more than the United Kingdom's total annual greenhouse gas emissions.
--- B
The practices involved are not new. Ploughing exposes organic matter to the air and oxidises it, so reducing tillage slows that loss. Long-term trials compiled in the journal Soil and Tillage Research found that sustained no-till farming raised the concentration of soil organic carbon in the top 30 centimetres by an average of 10 to 30 per cent over ten years, compared with conventional cultivation. Cover crops, sown between harvests, keep living roots in the ground; those roots release sugars that feed microbes, which convert organic material into humus, a form of carbon that persists. Cover crops also hold the soil in place, since erosion physically carries away the topsoil and the carbon inside it.
--- C
What makes soil carbon awkward as a commodity is that it cannot be seen. A tonne of carbon stored in a field looks exactly like a tonne that was never stored, it is distributed unevenly within a single field, and the annual change is small relative to the total stock. Buyers of credits therefore depend on measurement, reporting and verification, usually abbreviated to MRV, and the credibility of the entire market rests on how well that work is done.
--- D
Protocols have become stricter as a result. The Improved Soil Management protocol certified by Isometric requires removals to be measured by direct soil sampling on its own or together with validated modelling, and obliges projects to account for every associated emission, from tractor fuel to the manufacture of fertiliser. Each project must commit for between 40 and 100 years, and the certificates it issues are given a durability equal to half that commitment period. Landowners enrolled in a project must receive a minimum 20 per cent share of the revenue, and contributions to buffer pools, which exist to compensate for carbon lost elsewhere, vary with the reversal risk a project actually faces.
--- E
Measurement is also getting cheaper. A 2026 assessment by Sustainability Atlas concluded that satellite-based verification reduced the cost of measuring soil carbon by about 40 per cent relative to conventional field sampling, and investment in digital MRV reached 2.3 billion dollars in 2025. Optical sensors estimate biomass from reflected light, radar sees through cloud, and laser altimetry builds a three-dimensional picture of vegetation structure. None of these instruments measures carbon directly; they infer it from proxies, which is why registries still require them to be tied to samples taken from the ground.
--- F
Programmes are now operating at scale. AgreenaCarbon, registered under Verra's Verified Carbon Standard as project 4022 and using methodology VM0042, spans 4.5 million hectares across twenty markets and directs up to 85 per cent of the value of each credit sold back to the farms that produced it. In Ukraine, where war has disrupted ordinary agriculture, the enterprise Agrain applies the same model across more than 500 fields, using the income to keep production going and to protect the topsoil.
--- G
Bulgaria offers a detailed example of what verification involves. Carbonsafe's two projects there enrol 100 agricultural holdings covering 47,824 hectares, and had 45,480 and 423,411 credits respectively verified after soil monitoring at three depth intervals: 0 to 30, 30 to 60 and 60 to 90 centimetres. Removals are quantified by a measure-and-remeasure method based on laboratory analysis of field samples, with satellite data used to confirm that practices have been maintained. In August the rating agency BeZero Carbon visited the country to assess the methodology and the durability of the removals, a step that is routine for projects judged in advance but rare for ones backed by results that have already been measured.
--- H
The obstacle that remains is economic rather than scientific. Farmers must buy unfamiliar equipment, adjust established routines and accept uncertain yields for several years before soil fertility and income recover, and without carbon finance the transition may simply not be affordable. Permanence raises a second difficulty: soil carbon is not locked away for good, and a field that is ploughed again can release in a single season what a decade of careful management had added. The long commitment periods and the buffer pools written into current protocols are attempts to price that risk rather than to remove it.
