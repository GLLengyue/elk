--- A
Fusion has always been an attractive proposition and an elusive one. Join light atomic nuclei and energy is released without the long-lived waste that fission produces, and the fuel, isotopes of hydrogen, can be extracted from water. The difficulty is that the nuclei must be heated to temperatures found only in stars and held there long enough to react. For seventy years the joke has been that commercial fusion is three decades away and always will be. What changed in 2026 was not the physics but the money, the contracts and, increasingly, the hardware.
--- B
According to the Fusion Industry Association's 2026 survey, published in July, companies raised $4.48 billion in the twelve months to July 2026, the largest annual total since the survey began, bringing cumulative private investment since 2021 to $14.24 billion. Fifty-six firms were surveyed, against 23 five years earlier, and the sector employs more than 16,000 people. The year's largest single round came on 30 July, when Commonwealth Fusion Systems closed $1 billion, taking its own total to roughly $4 billion, or about 30 per cent of all private fusion capital raised to date. It was the biggest round since that company's $1.8 billion raise in 2021, and its backers included pension funds, sovereign wealth funds and infrastructure investors.
--- C
CFS is pursuing two machines in parallel. SPARC, at Devens in Massachusetts, is a compact tokamak that uses high-temperature superconducting magnets to confine the plasma; the company put it at roughly 80 per cent complete in 2026 and expects it to attempt scientific breakeven, in which the plasma yields more energy than is used to heat it, in 2027. The follow-on plant, ARC, is a 400-megawatt station planned for Chesterfield County in Virginia, for which the company applied in April 2026 to connect to the PJM Interconnection grid. Google has agreed to buy 200 megawatts of its output, about half the total, and Eni, the Italian energy company, has committed more than $1 billion. The company has not disclosed what either machine will cost.
--- D
CFS is not alone. Helion Energy raised $465 million in June 2026 and stated in February that its Polaris device had carried out a reaction using deuterium and tritium, heating plasma to 150 million degrees; the figures were published by the company itself and do not amount to a positive energy balance for the installation as a whole. In July the German firm Proxima Fusion closed $518 million, the largest fusion round in Europe, and Inertia Enterprises had raised $450 million in February.
--- E
The most frequently cited experimental result remains an American one. At the National Ignition Facility at Lawrence Livermore, powerful lasers compress a small fuel capsule; in December 2022 the reaction yielded more energy than the lasers delivered to the target, the first time this had been achieved anywhere. In an experiment in April 2025, 2.08 megajoules of laser energy produced 8.6 megajoules of fusion energy, a target gain of more than four and the eighth ignition at the facility, results the laboratory presented in April 2026. The achievement is real but narrow: the laser complex consumes far more electricity than reaches the capsule, and it fires single shots rather than running continuously.
--- F
The international ITER project in southern France, involving thirty-five nations, is designed to produce 500 megawatts of fusion power from 50 megawatts of external heating, a gain factor of ten, without generating any electricity. Assembly has continued: the central solenoid, made up of six modules, was completed in June 2026, and a sixth sector of the vacuum vessel was lowered into the pit in July, leaving roughly two-thirds of the ring in place. The schedule has slipped badly. Research operations are now expected in 2034, operation at full magnetic power in 2036, and the first deuterium-tritium experiments in 2039.
--- G
China's progress has been measured less in publications than in metal. On 27 June the Institute of Plasma Physics in Hefei completed full-performance testing of a D-shaped toroidal field coil that is by a wide margin the largest superconducting magnet ever built for a fusion reactor: 21 metres tall, 12 metres wide and 582 metric tons in weight, carrying a stable current of 60,000 amps. It has 1.3 times the volume and three times the stored energy of the equivalent ITER coil, took six years to build, and every significant input, from the specialty steel to the superconducting wire, was made in China. The coil is destined for BEST, a burning-plasma machine China hopes to complete around 2027.
--- H
What is being tested is no longer only whether fusion works. Investors who buy contracted, long-duration cash flows have concluded that a fusion plant can be underwritten like a terminal or a transmission line, and Chinese engineers have shown that reactor-scale hardware can be produced from a wholly domestic supply chain. The remaining obstacles are concrete rather than mysterious: materials that survive years of neutron bombardment, a supply of tritium that a fleet of reactors must breed for itself, and machines that must repeat in seconds what NIF manages once a day. None has been solved. What has changed is that the list of those willing to fund the attempt now includes pension funds.
