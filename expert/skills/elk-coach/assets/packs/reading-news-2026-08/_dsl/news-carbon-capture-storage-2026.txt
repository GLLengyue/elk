--- A
Carbon capture and storage occupies an unusual position in climate policy. It appears in almost every official route to net zero, yet it remains almost invisible in the real economy. According to the database maintained by the International Energy Agency, 75 CCS facilities were operating worldwide as of February 2026. Together they capture 62.5 million tonnes of CO2 each year, a volume roughly equal to the annual greenhouse gas output of Ecuador. Set against the 38.1 billion tonnes that fossil-fuel use emits annually, this amounts to less than 0.2 per cent of the problem the technology is meant to address.
--- B
The idea is not new. Cesare Marchetti proposed injecting captured CO2 underground in a paper published in 1977, and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change examined the subject in a special report in 2005, when only three small projects existed anywhere. A first wave of enthusiasm in the 2000s was directed mainly at coal-fired generation, but it faded as the cost of wind and solar collapsed and the case for keeping coal plants alive weakened. Today just seven coal-fired stations anywhere in the world have capture equipment attached: five in China, one in the United States and one in Canada.
--- C
What revived the technology was the Paris Agreement and the national net-zero targets that followed, which forced attention onto industries that had long assumed they would keep emitting. In steel and cement in particular, the IEA describes CCS as virtually the only technology capable of making deep cuts, because much of the CO2 released by these industries is a product of chemistry rather than combustion. Cement manufacture also has one incidental advantage: the exhaust leaving a cement kiln contains 14 to 20 per cent CO2 by volume, compared with 4 to 5 per cent at a gas-fired power station, and the richer stream makes solvent-based capture markedly cheaper.
--- D
That advantage is visible at Brevik in Norway, where Heidelberg Materials opened the first full-scale capture plant attached to a cement works in late 2024. Using amine scrubbing supplied by Aker Carbon Capture, it takes in about 400,000 tonnes of CO2 a year, roughly half the site's total emissions. The gas is liquefied, shipped out and injected beneath the North Sea through the Northern Lights storage project. The Norwegian state met around 80 per cent of the construction cost. Even so, the full chain of capturing, compressing, shipping and injecting adds $50-100 to the cost of each tonne of cement, which itself sells for $60-100.
--- E
Cost is dominated by one step. Capturing the gas accounts for 70 to 80 per cent of the total outlay of a CCS chain, because the energy needed to separate the CO2 and regenerate the solvent is considerable. Estimates compiled for the cement sector in 2026 put amine scrubbing at $50-80 per tonne, calcium looping at $40-60, oxy-fuel combustion at $60-90, and direct separation, which isolates the gas released by calcination, at $30-50. Chinese industry data assembled by Minsheng Securities point in the same direction but at lower absolute levels: integrated projects there were reported to have brought full-chain capture costs down to 250-300 yuan per tonne, with coal-chemical sources cheaper than power generation.
--- F
Performance in the field is another matter. The Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis has compiled data showing that operating plants frequently fall short of the 90 to 95 per cent capture rate that regulators treat as the baseline for a credible climate technology. Reported rates vary from as little as 17 per cent at steel facilities to around 80 per cent at hydrogen plants. The IEA's own net-zero scenario assumes that 1.7 gigatonnes will be captured annually by 2035, close to thirty times the current total. The agency counts 93.7 million tonnes of capacity under construction and a further 1,279.6 million tonnes at various planning stages.
--- G
A large share of what is captured does not, strictly speaking, stay out of the atmosphere for climate reasons alone. Roughly three-quarters of the CO2 handled by existing projects is pumped into depleted oil reservoirs to force out more crude, a practice known as enhanced oil recovery; most of the remainder is separated during natural gas purification. Critics argue that this makes CCS less a climate solution than a way of prolonging the life of fossil-fuel assets, and they note that the industry has a long record of announcing projects that are later cancelled or delayed.
--- H
The disagreement is ultimately about cost and opportunity. A 2023 working paper from the University of Oxford concluded that a pathway to net zero minimising the use of CCS would be about a trillion dollars a year cheaper than one relying on it heavily, and found no evidence of the steady cost reductions that learning curves have delivered in solar and batteries. Supporters reply that such comparisons assume alternatives exist for cement and steel, which in practice they largely do not. Britain has committed 21.7 billion pounds to the technology. Whether that buys a genuine abatement route or an expensive reprieve is likely to remain contested for years.
