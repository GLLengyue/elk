--- A
A carbon credit represents a verified reduction or removal of one tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent, recorded in a registry and cancelled, or retired, when it is used to support a claim. Companies buy them voluntarily, without being required by law to do so, usually because of a net zero commitment, pressure from investors, or a wish to finance mitigation beyond their own operations. The system is only as good as the word verified, which raises the questions that matter: which standard, which methodology, which auditor, which baseline, and which guarantee that the carbon stays out of the atmosphere.
--- B
Those questions became unavoidable in January 2023, when a joint investigation by The Guardian, the German weekly Die Zeit and the research organisation SourceMaterial alleged that many rainforest credits issued under Verra's methodologies for reducing emissions from deforestation did not represent the reductions claimed. Verra disputed the conclusions and challenged the methodology behind them. The scientific argument about baselines and counterfactual deforestation has never been settled, but the commercial reaction was immediate: corporate buyers grew cautious, claims of carbon-neutral products attracted scrutiny, and cheap avoidance credits lost much of the confidence that had carried them through the boom of 2020 and 2021.
--- C
Part of the difficulty is structural. The two largest registries, Verra and Gold Standard, between them issue the great majority of credits in the voluntary market. Both are non-profit organisations, which sounds neutral, but their certification fees are paid by the project developers whose projects they are certifying, and a developer cannot sell a credit until it has been certified. The payer and the certifier therefore want the same outcome. This does not prove that any particular credit is worthless; it does mean that certification is an interest-bearing judgement rather than an audit conducted on behalf of the buyer.
--- D
Events since have given buyers reason to look behind the certificate. In 2025 Verra cancelled more than 15 million credits linked to Kariba, a large forest project in Zimbabwe registered as VCS 902, after governance and integrity failures emerged, and suspended new issuances at a second project, Ecomapuá in Brazil. Katingan, a long-running project in Indonesia, has faced its own scrutiny over additionality, the question of whether the project would have happened anyway. Verra's share of retirements in the voluntary market fell below 60 per cent by early 2026, its lowest level since 2015, as buyers spread their purchases across other registries.
--- E
The market that has emerged from this is better understood as two overlapping ones. On one side sit higher-integrity credits: removal rather than avoidance, approved under stricter methodologies, sometimes authorised under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, and often carrying an independent rating. On the other sit legacy credits that may still be valid under a private standard but cannot safely support the same claim. Removal projects, which physically extract carbon dioxide and store it, are increasingly preferred to avoidance projects, which rest on a counterfactual guess about what would otherwise have happened. Buyers are also advised to look at permanence, at buffer arrangements that replace lost carbon, and at vintage, since credits issued under a decade-old methodology may reflect standards long since abandoned.
--- F
A further source of confusion is that several systems answer different questions, and are routinely mistaken for one another. The Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market assesses whether a programme or category meets its Core Carbon Principles. The Voluntary Carbon Markets Integrity Initiative is concerned with something else: what claim a company can credibly make when it uses credits. Article 6 of the Paris Agreement deals with whether a host country has authorised a credit for international use and adjusted its own accounts to avoid double counting. CORSIA, the aviation scheme, decides whether an airline may use a unit against its covered emissions. Independent raters such as Sylvera, BeZero Carbon and Calyx offer a separate view again, on additionality and project risk.
--- G
Prices reflect the resulting sorting. Over a recent period in 2026, credits in the BBB+ tier rose from $18.10 to $20.10 a tonne, while B-rated credits fell from $8.50 to $7.80 over the same window. A premium credit, in other words, is not one that has passed a single combined test; it is one whose buyer knows which of these frameworks it has passed and which it has not, and therefore what claim it can bear.
--- H
Regulation is now reinforcing the distinction. The European Union's Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive and tightening British advertising standards both bear on vague climate claims, and standards such as ISO 14068-1 require that emissions be reduced first and only the remainder offset. None of this makes credit purchase pointless: high-integrity strategies still use removal credits to neutralise the emissions that remain. But approval by a registry is the floor, not the ceiling, and the most important question in procurement is no longer whether a credit was certified. It is which of the two markets it came from.
