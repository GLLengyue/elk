--- A
Solar manufacturing has arrived at a peculiar position. The panels it produces have never been cheaper, and installations continue to set records, yet almost every major producer is losing money. The cause is arithmetic rather than technology: global demand for modules in 2026 is roughly 536 gigawatts, while the industry has built the capacity to make about 1,100 gigawatts. For a product that is largely standardised and hard to differentiate, the gap between those two numbers settles in only one place, which is the price.
--- B
Production is now contracting. According to the China Photovoltaic Industry Association, output across the main stages of the chain fell in the first half of 2026: polysilicon by 9.8 per cent to 538,000 tonnes, wafers by 7.3 per cent to 293 gigawatts, cells by 21.9 per cent to 260.7 gigawatts, and modules by 35.1 per cent to 201.3 gigawatts. The cuts have not restored profitability. Several of the largest listed manufacturers, including Longi, Tongwei, TCL Zhonghuan and Trina Solar, together forecast first-half losses exceeding 10 billion yuan.
--- C
Prices have fallen further and faster than output. The average price of dense polysilicon fell from 52 yuan per kilogram at the start of the year to 32.5 yuan by early July, and module tenders in China dropped below 0.7 yuan per watt. The clearest illustration comes from the grade of polysilicon used for N-type cells, which began 2026 at about 60,000 yuan a tonne and fell to around 32,000 by June. Industry estimates put the average full cost of production at roughly 48,000 yuan a tonne, with the most efficient makers managing 38,000 to 43,000, which means the market was clearing at 25 to 40 per cent below what it cost to make.
--- D
The origins of the surplus are not mysterious. Between 2021 and 2024 manufacturers expanded repeatedly on the assumption that demand would keep accelerating, and much of that capacity arrived just as growth in the largest market began to slow. China's dominance makes the consequences severe: by the end of 2025 it accounted for 96 per cent of global polysilicon capacity, 96.2 per cent of wafer capacity, 91.3 per cent of cell capacity and 80.1 per cent of module capacity. When a market that concentrated turns down, there is nowhere for the surplus to go.
--- E
Demand has weakened on two fronts. Domestic installations in China reached 72.07 gigawatts in the first half of 2026, down about 66 per cent on the previous year, following a rush to connect projects before policy changes. The association's former secretary-general Wang Bohua expects global installations of 612 gigawatts in 2026, some 8 per cent below the prior year, recovering to 864 gigawatts by 2030. Elsewhere, grid congestion, curtailment of solar output and increasingly frequent negative electricity prices have slowed the pace at which other markets can absorb modules.
--- F
Exports have been the release valve. Shipments of wafers, cells and modules brought in about 17.18 billion dollars in the first half of 2026, a rise of 24.3 per cent, helped by an export tax rebate. That route is narrowing. On 6 August 2026 an executive order in Washington imposed a minimum import price and a 15 per cent tariff on imported polysilicon and derived products, taking effect on 4 December. Producers in India and South-East Asia are expanding, and Indian surplus capacity is already being sold abroad, pulling down prices in third markets.
--- G
Chinese policy has shifted from persuasion to rules. On 2 July three ministries jointly issued mandatory national standards setting energy-consumption limits for polysilicon, wafers and modules, which effectively draws a line under the least efficient plant. On 27 July the China Photovoltaic Industry Association published the industry's first cost accounting standard, defining how costs should be calculated across the silicon feedstock, wafer, cell and module stages. A compliance meeting followed on 31 July, and on 6 August eight leading polysilicon makers signed a pledge not to sell below the full cost calculated under it.
--- H
The immediate effect was paralysis rather than recovery. For two consecutive weeks in August the polysilicon market recorded no transactions at all, as producers withdrew quotations rather than sign contracts they believed would breach the pledge. Stocks had reached about 522,000 tonnes by the end of July. Whether prices recover depends on whether inefficient capacity is genuinely retired rather than idled, and on whether demand returns: some analysts expect Chinese installations to resume growth in 2027 as grid reforms and storage deployment ease the constraints on connecting new plant.
