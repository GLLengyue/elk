--- A
Solar radiation management, or SRM, describes proposals for reflecting a small share of incoming sunlight back into space, most often by scattering particles in the stratosphere. It is not the same as carbon dioxide removal, and the distinction matters. Removing carbon dioxide tackles the accumulation of greenhouse gases; reflecting sunlight leaves that accumulation untouched. It therefore does nothing to slow ocean acidification, and treats one symptom of warming while the underlying cause continues to worsen.
--- B
What gives the idea its political charge is that it appears cheap and quick. The underlying chemistry is not speculative: large volcanic eruptions have been observed to cool the planet in exactly this way, and there is broad agreement that injecting reflective material would lower temperatures within months. Reflecting 1 per cent of incoming sunlight would, on the figures the industry cites, suffice to stabilise global temperature, and Stardust Solutions puts the cost of cooling the planet by about 0.5 degrees Celsius for a year at roughly $10 billion. An investor put it bluntly: if cooling the planet costs what one large computing campus costs, this is no longer something only a coalition of governments could attempt.
--- C
The company most closely associated with the push is Stardust Solutions, an American-Israeli venture that raised $75 million in 2025 and has been delivering sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere by balloon since 2022. It has developed a proprietary particle meant to be safer than sulphur: either amorphous silica or a silica core with a calcite shell, chosen to be inert and to biodegrade. It says it has also built a tagging technique able to follow each batch of particles as they disperse. What it has not done is publish peer-reviewed research or submit its claims to independent oversight, and its private funding is estimated to exceed the entire global public research budget for SRM.
--- D
That asymmetry is the heart of the problem. When the money available to one firm exceeds everything spent on public research, the shape of the technology, which particle, whose timeline, whose patents, is settled privately, and governments are left to ratify choices already made. Stardust initially scheduled open-air experiments for the spring of 2026 and then, under public pressure, stepped back, its chief executive saying it would proceed only with a government willing to set guardrails. The retreat was welcome, but it was voluntary; nothing compelled it.
--- E
The risks most often modelled are not failures of the machinery but changes to the climate itself. Simulations consistently indicate that stratospheric injection would alter atmospheric circulation, and the effects would not be evenly spread. The regions projected to suffer most are South and Southeast Asia, where the annual monsoon supplies water for agriculture and ecosystems across a population exceeding 2 billion. A weakened or erratic monsoon would threaten food production on a scale that could exceed the damage the intervention was meant to avert.
--- F
A second concern follows from the first. Because the method suppresses temperature rather than removing its cause, it must be maintained indefinitely; if injection were halted suddenly, by war, a collapse in funding or a change of government, the suppressed warming would return abruptly, far faster than the gradual warming to which societies have had decades to adapt. Researchers call this termination shock. Particles in the stratosphere may also affect the ozone layer, and the consequences of stopping cannot be tested without first having started.
--- G
No treaty governs any of this. There is at present no international legal framework for solar geoengineering, and the United Nations Environment Assembly earlier this year declined even to establish a study group on the subject. The United Nations Environment Programme has concluded that large-scale deployment is not currently necessary, practical, prudent or safe enough, while the United States National Academies have recommended careful research and oversight rather than uncontrolled deployment. India has become the first Asian government publicly to outline plans to regulate the technology, citing risks to its monsoon. Andy Parker, who directs a non-governmental organisation supporting SRM research in developing countries, has warned that the decisions are not going to be made in universities.
--- H
The confusion is not confined to legislatures. At a meeting of the Israeli parliament's Interior and Environmental Protection Committee on 30 June 2026, the legitimate question of how to license field trials was crowded out by claims that aircraft were already spreading chemicals over the country. Officials stated that no such activity was known to be taking place, and no evidence was presented. A real and contested field of research had been merged, in a parliamentary chamber, with a conspiracy theory that has no support.
--- I
None of this is an argument for abandoning the research. A technology that might spare millions of people the worst effects of warming deserves to be studied, and studied openly, with published results and independent monitoring. The objection is narrower and harder to dismiss: outdoor experiments that could alter rainfall on another continent are being prepared by a company answerable to its shareholders, in the absence of any body with authority to say no. The danger is not that SRM will fail to work. It is that it will work, and that nobody will have agreed in advance on who decides.
