--- A
For two decades microplastics were treated as an environmental problem, a pollutant in oceans and soil. A perspective published on 5 May 2026 in the first issue of the journal Brain Health argues that the framing is now untenable. An international group of researchers, from the University of New Mexico, the University of Ottawa, TU Dresden and King's College London, set out to combine three lines of evidence that had developed separately, and concluded that plastic accumulation in the human brain should be treated as a medical emergency.
--- B
The tissue evidence is the most striking part of the argument. Researchers at the University of New Mexico analysed brain, liver and kidney samples taken from donors who died between 2016 and 2024, and found that plastic concentrations in the brain were seven to thirty times higher than in matched liver or kidney samples. The cumulative plastic burden in brain tissue rose by approximately 50 per cent between 2016 and 2024, and donors with a diagnosis of dementia carried the heaviest loads. Polyethylene, mostly in nanoscale shard-like fragments, was the predominant plastic. Even so, the authors stopped short of claiming that the particles had caused the disease: the samples show accumulation, not mechanism.
--- C
A second line of evidence comes from cardiology. A study published in the New England Journal of Medicine followed 257 patients who had undergone surgery to remove plaque from the carotid arteries supplying the brain. Polyethylene was detected in 58.4 per cent of the plaque samples and polyvinyl chloride in a further 12.1 per cent. Over nearly 34 months of follow-up, patients whose plaque contained these particles faced a composite risk of heart attack, stroke or death roughly four and a half times higher than those whose plaque did not. The authors' point is that this is as much a brain finding as a cardiac one, since a stroke is an outcome in the brain.
--- D
The question of how the particles arrive there is being answered in animals. Work by Kopatz and colleagues showed that nanoscale polystyrene particles given to mice by mouth crossed the blood-brain barrier within two hours, apparently using the coating of biological molecules they acquire in the blood as a kind of passport; larger particles did not cross. Other experiments have shown immune cells engulfing nanoplastics and then obstructing the fine capillaries of the brain, reducing cerebral blood flow, and rats dosed with nanoplastics before an induced stroke suffered greater inflammation, more neuron death in the hippocampus and worse neurological performance.
--- E
Exposure is continuous and comes by several routes at once. Ultra-processed foods, which supply more than half of all calories consumed in the United States, are thought to be a major delivery mechanism, because food repeatedly contacts plastic packaging and machinery during manufacture; one meta-analysis of 385,541 participants linked the highest intake of such foods to 53 per cent higher odds of common mental disorder symptoms. Eighty-seven per cent of tap water samples across 34 countries contained microplastics, mostly polyester fragments below 50 micrometres. Researchers at Zhengzhou University estimate that inhalation alone may account for close to half of total intake: particles under 2.5 micrometres can reach the alveoli, and human lung tissue has been found to hold between 0.56 and 6.00 particles per gram.
--- F
What the field cannot yet do is say how much of this matters. A systematic review in the Journal of Xenobiotics, which searched four databases in October 2025 and included fourteen studies, found microplastics in blood, vascular tissue, thrombi and arterial plaque, and reported possible associations with heart attack, stroke, hypertension and clotting. It also concluded that the certainty of the evidence was very low and that most of the studies carried a high or very high risk of bias. One study found plastic in 80 per cent of the blood clots analysed, but an association of that kind cannot establish whether the plastic contributed to the clot or merely accumulated in it.
--- G
There is, meanwhile, no clinical machinery for dealing with any of it. Western medicine has produced no clinical guidance, dietary recommendations or standard testing protocols for measuring plastic burden in a patient, so neither doctors nor individuals can find out how much they carry. Research agencies have begun to respond: a United States biomedical agency has launched a programme on the systematic removal of microplastics, and a blood-filtering technique long used for other conditions has been shown to clear particles from plasma. Engineering solutions have their own obstacles: researchers at Fuzhou University found that when perfluorooctanoic acid is present alongside the plastic, the efficiency of electrochemical breakdown falls by more than half, because the acid competes for the reactive oxygen that drives the process.
--- H
That combination, rather than any single result, is what gives the argument its force, and also what limits it. Each finding is individually open to objection: tissue samples cannot establish causation, animal experiments may not transfer to people, and dietary studies cannot separate plastic from the rest of a processed diet. Taken together, however, they describe a contaminant that is present in the organ with the least capacity to repair itself, that is increasing, and that nobody is measuring. The authors called for urgent attention, for standardised detection methods and for prospective studies able to separate correlation from cause. Until those exist, the only advice available is unglamorous: reduce exposure where it is cheapest to do so.
