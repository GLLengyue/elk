--- A
Specialists draw a sharp distinction between hazard and risk. Hazard describes the conditions that make fire likely to start and spread: dry vegetation, strong winds, temperatures raised by climate change. Risk describes where people have chosen to live and how the land around them is managed. By late August 2026 more than 900 fires were burning across Canada and some 3.7 million hectares had burned. Martin Girardin, a forest ecologist with the Canadian Forest Service, was blunt about the limits of response: asking crews to extinguish a fire of that size, he said, is rather like trying to stop a hurricane. Hazard is driven by forces no single country controls; risk, by contrast, is in principle entirely within our power to reduce.
--- B
For most of human history fire was a tool rather than an enemy. Indigenous and traditional communities on every inhabited continent developed systems for putting flame on the ground at particular seasons and in particular places. Colonial administrations dismantled them. In California, Spanish missionaries punished Indigenous people for setting fires as early as 1793, a prohibition the state legislature renewed in 1850; in Australia, British settlers suppressed Aboriginal burning. Research by the World Resources Institute and WWF-Australia finds that Indigenous-managed lands consistently lose less tree cover, store more carbon and support more biodiversity than comparable surrounding areas when communities hold genuine authority over fire management.
--- C
Two related practices are being revived under different names. Prescribed burning is the deliberate ignition of low- to moderate-intensity fires, usually before the season begins, to clear accumulated dry vegetation; burning in patches also breaks the landscape into a mosaic that can slow a later blaze. Cultural burning rests on the same physical principle but carries a different purpose. Amy Cardinal Christianson of the Indigenous Leadership Initiative notes that much of the burning her communities did historically took place around settlements and places they valued. This is not nostalgia; it is ecological insight that measurement is only now catching up with.
--- D
The evidence for both is accumulating. A Stanford-led study drawing on two decades of satellite observations concluded that California could cut smoke-related mortality in active fire years by about 20 per cent by expanding prescribed fire in conifer forests, though the authors did not claim the technique removes exposure; it reduces it, and only where applied at scale. Research summarised in May 2026 found that every dollar the United States Forest Service spent on reducing wildfire risk prevented nearly four dollars of damage. In Arnhem Land an Aboriginal savanna burning programme both lowers emissions and sells carbon credits.
--- E
Protecting settlements means attending to the buildings themselves. FireSmart, a framework founded in 1993 and now operating in every Canadian province and territory, advises on fire-resistant materials, safe storage of firewood and individual property assessments. Its limits were made plain in Jasper, where about 30 per cent of the town burned in 2024. Fire chief Mathew Conte observed that the destroyed area contained houses built close to conifers, tightly spaced and topped with combustible roofs. One household's precautions, he noted, achieve little if a neighbour's do not: a neighbourhood is only as strong as its weakest link. Rebuilding has focused on education, sprinklers and critical infrastructure.
--- F
The second line of defence is the fuel break, known in Canada as a fireguard: a band around a settlement where vegetation is cleared or thinned so that an approaching fire has less to consume. Whitehorse has been building one since 2020, and Alberta's Forest Resource Improvement Association started a community programme in 2023. The Bow Valley Community Fireguard, being built outside Canmore near Banff National Park, is about halfway complete. Fire chief Shawn Polley is careful about what such works achieve: they do not stop a fire, they slow it and give crews a safer place to stand.
--- G
Institutions are changing too. California's governor issued an emergency proclamation in March 2025 creating a fast-track route for forest management and fuel reduction projects that had previously required years of environmental review; by June 2026, 400 had been approved through it. In March 2026 the state's Natural Resources Agency issued California's first Tribal Stewardship Policy, setting a goal of expanding tribal stewardship over at least 7.5 million acres of land and coastal waters. A draft five-year plan released in June was open for comment until 7 August.
--- H
Detection and staffing have been transformed. CAL FIRE, responsible for 31 million acres, has added an average of 1,800 permanent and 600 seasonal posts a year over the past five years, roughly double the previous pace. The Fire Integrated Real-Time Intelligence System has flown more than 2,000 missions since 2019. Cameras on mountain tops use machine learning to identify smoke plumes and alert dispatchers before emergency calls arrive, and on 7 July 2026 the state launched FireSat, satellites able to detect new ignitions from orbit within minutes. A campaign called One Less Spark, begun on 15 July 2026, targets the behaviour behind an estimated 95 per cent of California's wildfires.
--- I
None of this alters who bears the consequences. First Nations people account for roughly 42 per cent of wildfire-related evacuations in Canada while making up about 5 per cent of the population, and by mid-July more than 4,300 had been evacuated. The burden falls on those with least control over land-use decisions. The evidence assembled here points away from the customary response: money committed before a fire starts buys more protection than money committed after, and the cheapest hectare to treat is the one that has not yet ignited.
