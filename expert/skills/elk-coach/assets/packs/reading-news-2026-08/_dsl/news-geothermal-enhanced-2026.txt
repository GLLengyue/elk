--- A
For most of its history, geothermal power has been a matter of luck. Conventional plants need a rare combination of heat, permeable rock and underground water, found naturally in only a handful of places such as Iceland and parts of the western United States. Enhanced geothermal systems, usually shortened to EGS, are an attempt to stop relying on that coincidence. Rather than search for a reservoir that already exists, engineers create one, fracturing hot rock deep underground and circulating water through the cracks. If the approach works, the resource ceases to be local.
--- B
The method is not new in conception but it is new in execution, and what changed it was the shale gas boom. Horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing, developed to extract oil and gas from impermeable rock, turned out to be directly applicable to geothermal wells. A vertical shaft is sunk to the target depth, the bit is steered horizontally through the hot formation, and water is pumped in at high pressure to open a network of fractures. A second well, drilled to intersect the first, returns the heated water to the surface, where the heat is extracted and the cooled water injected back down to repeat the cycle.
--- C
Progress in drilling has been the most visible measure of whether this is working. In July 2026 Fervo Energy, the company most closely associated with the technology, reported that its ninth third-generation well, Sawtooth 7, had reached a measured depth of 19,448 feet in 21 days, including a horizontal section of 7,500 feet, in rock at 460 degrees Fahrenheit. The comparison the company drew was with its own first commercial well, drilled in 2022 at Project Red: 11,220 feet in 70 days. On that basis drilling time had fallen by about 70 per cent and the rate of drilling had risen by 143 per cent.
--- D
Behind those numbers is a sequence of well designs. The first generation, tested at Project Red, yielded roughly 3 megawatts per well at an implied capital cost of more than $15,000 per kilowatt. The second generation, used for the first phase of Cape Station, extended the horizontal section to about 5,000 feet in rock at 400 degrees Fahrenheit. The third generation, of which Sawtooth 7 is the ninth example, produces roughly 15 megawatts per well at about $5,500 per kilowatt, and the company has set a longer-term target of $3,000 per kilowatt. Its specification for wells drilled at scale is more ambitious still: above 25 megawatts per well, from horizontal sections of up to 15,000 feet in rock hotter than 500 degrees.
--- E
Cape Station, Fervo's flagship project in southern Utah, is being built in blocks. The first phase consists of three units the company calls GeoBlocks, together rated at approximately 100 megawatts, and the first of these is expected to begin generating electricity in the final quarter of 2026. A second phase, adding about 400 megawatts, is scheduled for delivery in 2028. Financing for the first phase was completed in the opening months of 2026 with $421 million of non-recourse project debt, a structure that lenders generally reserve for assets whose cash flows they consider reasonably predictable.
--- F
The spending required to get there dwarfs what the company currently earns. In the second quarter of 2026 Fervo recorded revenue of only $110,000 against a net loss of $55.9 million, an operating loss of $28.7 million and capital expenditure of $226.5 million. It could do so because of the money raised at its stock market flotation in May 2026, roughly $2.2 billion, of which about $2.1 billion remained in cash. On the strength of that balance sheet the company raised its target for installed capacity in 2030 from 1 gigawatt to 1.1 gigawatts, and reports a development pipeline exceeding 50 gigawatts on land rights covering more than 650,000 acres.
--- G
What makes the arithmetic plausible is who is buying. Fervo reports a backlog of about $7.2 billion and binding power purchase agreements covering 658 megawatts. Roughly half of that contracted capacity is committed to hyperscale data centres, 35 per cent to utilities and 15 per cent to industrial customers. The significance of the first category is not simply volume but shape: operators of large computing facilities want power that is available every hour of the day, and are reportedly prepared to pay a premium for it. Wind and solar, which produce intermittently, cannot supply that on their own without storage.
--- H
Sceptics raise two objections. The first is geological rather than financial: unlike a solar panel, whose output can be calculated in advance, an enhanced geothermal reservoir must be drilled before its productivity is known, and reservoirs can cool faster than models predict as heat is drawn off more quickly than it is replaced. The second is that fracturing rock at depth can induce small earthquakes, a problem that has dogged earlier projects elsewhere. Whether Fervo's particular geology behaves as its models assume will not be settled by drilling records or contract announcements, but by the output of the operating plant.
