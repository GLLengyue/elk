--- A
In the past two decades the trillions of bacteria that live inside the human gut have moved from a medical curiosity to a central character in the story of health. The community, collectively known as the gut microbiome, has been linked to digestion, immunity, mood and even the response to cancer treatment. Yet for all this attention, one question has proved stubbornly hard to answer: if the bacteria in our intestines are shaped by what we eat, why can we not simply read a person's gut and prescribe a diet?
--- B
A study published in Nature Medicine in 2026 brought the field closer to an answer. Researchers working with a cohort of more than ten thousand adults in Israel drew on app-based food diaries and shotgun metagenomic sequencing, which reads the genetic material of every organism in a sample at once. Rather than looking at broad categories of bacteria, they worked at the level of individual species, the finest point at which a microbe can be identified. Accounting for age, sex, body-mass index and underlying illness, diet predicted the abundance of nine in ten of the gut species they tested, and of ninety-eight per cent of the metabolic pathways they measured.
--- C
The most striking finding concerned what mattered most. The researchers did not find that the split between a plant-rich and an animal-rich diet was the dominant influence on microbial diversity. Instead, the degree to which a food had been processed predicted diversity more strongly. Minimally processed, nutrient-dense food was the strongest positive predictor of the range of species present, while ultra-processed food was the strongest negative predictor. In practical terms, this means that cutting back on industrially refined products may do more for the gut than swapping meat for vegetables.
--- D
The same study mapped the diet onto the members of the community in a way that had previously been impossible. Coffee appeared to be linked to the abundance of a species that produces butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid associated with a healthy gut lining. Yogurt was associated with a species common in fermented dairy, while dairy products and bread were tied to two closely related members of a genus that dominates the gut in infancy. Nuts and bananas were strong predictors of the abundance of particular species within two families of fibre-fermenting bacteria. Among the gut bacteria most often predicted to change with a shift in one or two foods were Akkermansia muciniphila and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, both of which have been linked to cardiometabolic markers. These links are associations rather than proof of cause, but they are specific enough to begin guiding advice.
--- E
A separate strand of research asks how such knowledge might be turned into a product rather than an abstract guideline. A review led by investigators at a university dental school in Philadelphia, published in August 2026, examined fermented plant-based foods such as kimchi and sauerkraut. These foods, the authors argue, differ from the liquid probiotic drinks already on the market. A yogurt typically carries a small number of bacterial strains from a defined starter culture; a fermented vegetable carries a much wider community of native microbes, together with the metabolites they have produced.
--- F
The review also pointed to a physical reason why solid fermented food may behave differently. Its fibrous structure protects the microorganisms it carries and lets them remain longer on the moist surfaces of the mouth and gut, creating more opportunities for interaction than a drink, which passes through quickly. Beyond the live bacteria themselves, plant-based fermented food brings dietary fibre and bioactive plant compounds in the same package. The authors are careful to stress that these foods are not being proposed as treatments, but as candidates for controlled testing.
--- G
To move from either line of research to actual advice, the authors of the Nature Medicine study argue that randomised trials are the essential next step. Observation can show that certain foods and certain microbes move together; only an experiment can show whether changing one changes the other in a predictable way. In the meantime, several practical priorities have been set out: building more comprehensive databases of the organisms present in fermented foods, developing better tools for profiling microbial strains and metabolites, and designing products with a defined microbial and chemical composition.
--- H
These ambitions, however, carry a caution. The specificity that makes microbiome research exciting also makes its findings fragile. A link between one food and one species in a large cohort may not hold for a single individual, whose microbiome differs from every other person's. And associations, however precisely measured, cannot distinguish a food that supports a microbe from one that merely happens to be eaten alongside it. The gap between a promising observation and a dependable recommendation remains wide, and both groups of researchers acknowledge it.
